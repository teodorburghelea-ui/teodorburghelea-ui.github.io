Research

Flow meets microstructure

Complex fluids are built from units much larger and slower than molecules. When they flow, the flow rearranges those units, and the rearranged structure feeds back on the flow.

We study that two-way coupling in polymer solutions, yield-stress gels, polymer melts, suspensions and phase-change materials. Most of what we find comes down to a transition, and we treat it with the tools of nonlinear dynamics and critical phenomena. The applications are transport: mixing, heat transfer and emulsification.

The lens

Why a physicist of phase transitions studies hair gel

Complex fluids span huge ranges of length and time scales (Fig. 1). Their transport of mass, heat and momentum is tied to their microstructure (Fig. 2). Constitutive models compress this into switches: a yield stress, a critical Weissenberg number, a stability limit. Careful experiments keep showing something richer, with coexisting phases, hysteresis, critical slowing down and imperfect bifurcations. Four ideas from statistical and nonlinear physics recur throughout the work below.

Imperfect bifurcations

Landau-type descriptions of the onsets of elastic turbulence, convection in yield-stress gels, draw resonance and drop trapping.

Phase coexistence & hysteresis

Yielding passes through a solid–fluid coexistence regime, much as magnetisation does in a ferromagnet.

Statistical models

A Gibbs-field (Ising-type) model of yielding, with a critical interaction strength that plays the role of a Curie point.

Smooth chaos

Batchelor-regime scalar turbulence, finite-time Lyapunov exponents and Lagrangian statistics for mixing and heat transport.

Idea 01

Elastic turbulence: turbulence without inertia

Accepted picture

At vanishing Reynolds number, flows are laminar, and microchannels can mix only by slow molecular diffusion.

What we showed

A trace of flexible polymer makes microflows chaotic. They mix in the Batchelor regime, carry heat up to four times better, and enter turbulence without hysteresis.

Adding a small amount of flexible polymer to a viscous liquid can make its flow chaotic even when inertia is negligible. The elastic stresses stored in the stretched polymers feed back on the flow and drive a random, turbulence-like motion: elastic turbulence. This line of research began with Teo’s PhD at the Weizmann Institute of Science with Victor Steinberg. That work characterised the role of elastic stress, the elastic boundary layer, the Lagrangian statistics of the flow and the breakdown of the Taylor hypothesis. It also gave the first quantitative test of the decay regime of mixing in a random, spatially smooth flow, including the anomalous scaling of the mixing length with the Péclet number.

At LTeN we turned it into a transport tool. Elastic turbulence raises heat transfer up to fourfold, comparable to a Newtonian flow at Re ≈ 2000, and temperature is advected as a passive scalar. In a serpentine microchannel the transition proved to be an imperfect bifurcation rather than the expected subcritical one. Near onset there are rare “flight–crash” events, in which the whole flow decelerates (see the movie on the home page). Velocity-gradient fluctuations saturate about 20 times above the theoretical prediction, a puzzle we attribute to confinement. Recent simulations of the 2D Taylor–Couette flow, with Lille, confirm a non-hysteretic onset and show that the dynamics are confined to a boundary layer near the inner wall.

Idea 02

Yielding: a transition, not a number

Accepted picture

Carbopol is a “model” yield-stress fluid: a Herschel–Bulkley material that switches sharply and reversibly from solid to liquid at τy.

What we showed

Yielding is gradual, hysteretic and mediated by elasticity, through a solid–fluid coexistence regime. Beyond a critical interaction strength it becomes irreversible even under steady forcing.

Many materials respond to stress in two ways. At low stress they behave like solids: they may deform, but they do not flow. Above the yield stress they flow like (usually non-Newtonian) fluids. Food pastes, hair gels, cosmetics, cement and mud all belong to this class.

From the “yield stress myth” to a phase transition

People: Eliane Younes, Cathy Castelain, Teo Burghelea · Collaborators: Volfango Bertola (Liverpool), Miguel Moyers-Gonzalez (Canterbury), Raazesh Sainudiin (Uppsala), Zdeněk Starý and Michal Himl (Prague)

Simple experiments with Carbopol do not fit the model picture. A settling sphere breaks fore–aft symmetry and leaves a negative wake. A plate withdrawn from the gel does not behave as expected. Careful stress ramps show why: between solid (S) and fluid (F) there is a regime where solid and fluid coexist (S+F), the loading and unloading branches differ, and the gel recoils elastically. The hysteresis grows with the rate at which the material is forced.

We described this first with a minimal microstructural model (Rheol. Acta 2009) and confirmed it in pipe flow (2014). We then derived it from statistical physics: a Gibbs random-field model, close to the Ising model, and its reduced nonlinear dynamical system (Soft Matter 2015, 2017). Their central prediction is a critical interaction strength between the microscopic units, the analogue of a Curie point, beyond which yielding is irreversible however slowly the material is forced. We have since seen the coexistence directly by fluorescently staining the gel during flow (2021). We found the same behaviour in concentrated xanthan, including a new “molten solid” state (Soft Matter 2024), and tested tensorial elasto-viscoplastic models against it (2025).

Confined flows and wall slip

People: Eliane Younes, Cathy Castelain, Teo Burghelea · Collaborators: Volfango Bertola, Miguel Moyers-Gonzalez

In a microchannel, the structure of a physical gel such as Carbopol is strongly heterogeneous. The classical picture of wall slip, a solvent layer of constant thickness, is therefore not justified. We relate macroscopic rheology directly to velocity gradients measured at the wall, with no assumption about the flow structure. The resulting slip scaling laws differ substantially from earlier ones (Phys. Rev. Fluids 5, 083303, 2020).

Idea 03

Instabilities without inertia

Accepted picture

Slow, viscous flows are stable. In a yield-stress fluid, the yield stress suppresses convection.

What we showed

A strongly stratified field destabilises inertia-free flows. It can be viscosity, elastic stress, crystal content or temperature.

Heating a layer from below or stratifying it by density can set it in motion with no help from inertia. The same holds for any field that enters the momentum balance and varies strongly in space. We found examples across very different materials: internal viscoelastic waves sustained by stratified hoop stresses in a Couette flow (2002), and a shear instability triggered by a fast acid–base reaction that creates a Carbopol gel at the interface between two streams (2007, 2011). The second improves displacement and mixing at low Re. Rayleigh–Bénard convection does develop in Carbopol, contrary to theoretical expectations. It emerges as an imperfect bifurcation with critical slowing down, controlled by a yield number rather than a Rayleigh number (2014).

Yielding to heat: chaos in a melting pot

Project: Exprodil, coordinated by Cathy Castelain · People: Rawad Himo, Cathy Castelain, Teo Burghelea

Phase-change materials yield to heat rather than stress: solid below their melting point, fluid above it. What happens to the stability of such a material when it is cooled slowly while being sheared? Just below the onset of flow-induced crystallisation, the crystals drive a bifurcation to an oscillatory flow. Further cooling makes the flow fully chaotic, which we confirmed with the 0–1 test for chaos (J. Fluid Mech. 918, A47, 2021).

Rayleigh–Taylor instability in complex fluids

A heavier fluid on top of a lighter one is unstable. We study how tuning the rheology of the two fluids controls the onset and development of this instability, combining table-top experiments with simulations in the Gerris flow solver. One example is a strongly shear-thinning top fluid (power-law index n = 0.45) over a Newtonian bottom fluid about ten times less viscous.

Idea 04

What you see is what you get: measurement and polymer melts

Accepted picture

The maximum (“overshoot”) of the extensional viscosity of LDPE is a molecular property that models should reproduce.

What we showed

The overshoot appears exactly when secondary necks form. It is a macroscopic instability, so the integral measurement is only valid while deformation stays homogeneous.

Extrusion, film casting, fibre spinning and blow moulding all involve strong, mostly extensional flows of molten polymers. With Helmut Münstedt in Erlangen, we added real-time imaging to a Münstedt extensional rheometer. This gives local strain rates and local stresses along the sample, a kinematic and dynamic fingerprint of how homogeneous the test is. The method showed that integral extensional viscosities of inhomogeneous samples are unreliable. It also resolved the overshoot debate, and mapped the viscous, transitional and elastic regimes of necking and rupture. The same habit of seeing the flow explains sharkskin, draw resonance in film casting (stable, oscillatory and chaotic regimes), and the faster crystallisation of polypropylene under extension compared with shear. The extension work was done with Zdeněk Starý (Czech Academy of Sciences), and the crystallisation work at LTeN with Nicolas Boyard.

  • On the “viscosity overshoot” during the uniaxial extension of a low density polyethylene, J. Non-Newton. Fluid Mech. 166, 1198 (2011)

Idea 05

Mixing and dispersing what does not want to mix

Accepted picture

Laminar chaotic advection mixes any viscous fluid if the stirring protocol is right.

What we showed

Rheology changes the answer. A yield stress makes mixing fail abruptly, shear thinning makes it better, and polymer additives make turbulent emulsification cheaper.

An in-line mixer with rotating arc-walls

Project: ANR Naymis, coordinated by Yves Le Guer · People: Eliane Younes, Mosbah Kiwan, Cathy Castelain, Kamal El Omari, Yann Moguen, Teo Burghelea

In highly viscous fluids, molecular diffusion is slow and mixing times are very long. This is a real problem for the cosmetic, food and polymer-processing industries. We designed an in-line mixer whose walls contain three arcs that rotate back and forth with the same amplitude but different phases. It creates chaotic advection at low Reynolds number, and we characterised it in the Eulerian and Lagrangian frames (finite-size Lyapunov exponents, residence times).

Yield stress: a catastrophic failure of mixing

Above a critical Bingham number, Bn ≈ 5, mixing efficiency and homogeneity collapse even though the flow and the stirring protocol are unchanged. Unyielded blobs add a new length scale to the problem. Their signature is a peak in the scalar spectrum that blocks the Batchelor cascade, and the material is “frozen” once it leaves the active region (Theor. Comput. Fluid Dyn. 37, 2023).

Shear thinning: a help, not a hindrance

People: Teo Burghelea (playing as both student and advisor…)

We simulate the arc-wall mixer with the Gerris flow solver at fixed Re = 1, St = 1 and Pe = 107, lowering the power-law index from n = 1 (Newtonian) to n = 0.2. We start from a forcing protocol that mixes poorly in the Newtonian case. With the same dimensionless numbers, shear thinning greatly enhances mixing, because it confines the wall forcing (two manuscripts submitted, 2026).

Drag-reduction-assisted emulsification

People: Elia Missi, Agnès Montillet, Jérôme Bellettre, Teo Burghelea · Collaborators: Yongbin Ji, Patrizio Massoli (CNR Napoli)

In a turbulent microfluidic cross-slot (Re up to 16,000), a minute amount of xanthan in the continuous phase reduces drag and still produces fine emulsions, at a much lower energy cost. High-speed imaging of single drops shows two dynamic modes, trapping in the impingement region and break-up. Both depend on the flow rate and on the shear-thinning rheology.

Application

Suspensions of micro-algae

Micro-algae are grown in photobioreactors to produce biofuels and high-value compounds. Their suspensions are complex fluids, and their rheology and flow affect how well the reactors work. With Jérémy Pruvost and colleagues at GEPEA, we characterised suspensions of Chlorella vulgaris across volume fractions. As the fraction rises they move from Newtonian to shear-thinning to an apparent yield stress with thixotropy. In microchannels they show wall slip and strong hydrodynamic irreversibility, which points back to the same yielding physics described above. Current PhD work by Masaya Hashimoto focuses on gentle fuel extraction from the colonial micro-alga Botryococcus braunii.

Key papers
  • Rheological properties of suspensions of the green microalga Chlorella vulgaris at various volume fractions, Rheol. Acta 52, 589–605 (2013)
  • Microscopic flows of suspensions of the green non-motile Chlorella micro-alga at various volume fractions, J. Non-Newton. Fluid Mech. 231, 91–101 (2016)
  • Investigation and modeling of the effects of light spectrum and incident angle on the growth of Chlorella vulgaris in photobioreactors, Biotechnol. Prog. 32, 247 (2016)

Programme

Open questions

The next step is consolidation: turning well-documented observations into a predictive understanding that holds across materials.

  • Closing the loop on yielding

    Use microstructure statistics measured in situ (solid fractions, correlation lengths) to fix the parameters of the Gibbs-field and reduced models, then test them in non-rheometric flows with tensorial simulations.

  • Is elasto-viscoplastic yielding universal?

    Carbopol, dense micro-algae and entangled xanthan all yield through hysteresis. A cross-material comparison should separate what is universal from what is chemistry, and identify the order parameter.

  • Elastic turbulence under confinement

    Why do velocity-gradient fluctuations saturate about 20× above theory? Boundary layers, walls and the information content of elastic-turbulent fields, studied experimentally and numerically.

  • Design rules for mixing structured fluids

    Predict and control mixing and emulsification of shear-thinning, viscoelastic and yield-stress materials, from in-line mixers to microfluidics and bioprocessing.
